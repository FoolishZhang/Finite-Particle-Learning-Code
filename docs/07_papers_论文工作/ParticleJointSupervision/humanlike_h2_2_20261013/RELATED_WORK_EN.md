**Monte Carlo objectives and simulated likelihood.** The logarithm of an unbiased likelihood estimator is biased, and the bias depends on the parameters. This is established for simulated maximum likelihood in discrete-choice models [@lee1995], for Monte Carlo likelihood inference, where increasing simulation accuracy inside the logarithm and averaging a fixed-$K$ log objective are distinct limits [@sung2007], and for Monte Carlo variational objectives [@maddison2017]. For a nonnegative unbiased normalizing-constant estimator, AESMC's Claim 1 writes the expected-log gap as a KL divergence on an extended sampling space [@le2018aesmc]; that divergence is not the observation risk of the integrated model studied here. For dependence specifically, the abstract of @geweke1997 reports downward bias of serial-correlation estimates in GHK simulated-likelihood experiments; we treat this as an empirical precedent, not as something our mechanism explains.

**Weight degeneracy and gradient asymptotics.** High-dimensional importance sampling [@agapiou2017; @chatterjee2018], particle-filter collapse [@bengtsson2008; @bickel2008; @snyder2008] and multi-sample gradient estimators [@rainforth2018; @tucker2019] are well studied. The large-$K$ scale $w_{n,K}$ matches the expected maximum-weight excess in @bickel2008; dependence selection requires control at that scale. The nearest result to Theorem 1 is @daudel2026 (Section D.4.1, equation (208)). Under a Gaussian random-field condition on the log-weights, and with derivative--expectation interchange, the subleading mean gradient follows the derivative of the log-weight standard deviation. Through the conditional-variance identity of Section 3, that formula formally predicts our large-$K$ coefficient. Its proof uses independence between the standardized log-weight field and its parameter derivative, which approximate Gaussianity does not preserve (their Section 3.4). @daudel2023 (Theorem 6) treats non-Gaussian iid log-weight sums and gives scalar objective asymptotics; that scalar remainder cannot simply be differentiated into a correlation field that is uniform at the smaller selection scale. What Theorem 1 adds is this control for the actual non-Gaussian weights, uniform over the correlation class and over $2\le K\le C_0n^p$, including every fixed $K\ge2$. Theorem 2 then combines it with the classical extreme-point geometry of correlation matrices [@litam1994] to reach an observation-risk conclusion. Appendix B gives a theorem-level comparison.

**Latent-variable learning and local geometry.** Learning binary latent models by prior-sampled likelihood predates this work [@tipping1998]. Importance-weighted autoencoders change their learned representations when the objective changes [@burda2016, Section 5.2 and Table 2]; our criterion is the negative of their bound with the proposal fixed to the prior. A learned-proposal version is used for item factor analysis [@urban2021]. Collapse and local stability have been analysed in linear latent-variable models [@lucas2019; @wangziyin2022]; for linear VAEs, @lucas2019 (Theorem 1) rule out additional ELBO local maxima relative to the marginal likelihood, and fixing the observation variance affects pPCA stability. Theorem 3 instead certifies, in a nonlinear model, a full-space local minimum of a finite-particle objective with a marginal-preserving direction along which it opposes a risk improvement; it is not a claim about low rank in general.
